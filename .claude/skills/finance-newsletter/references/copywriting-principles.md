# Copywriting Principles for Finance & Banking Newsletters

## Core framework: AIDA, adapted for finance

- **Attention** — subject line + preheader. In finance, attention is best earned through specificity and relevance, not hype. "3 rate changes that affect your mortgage this month" outperforms "Big news inside!" because it signals a real, personal stake.
- **Interest** — hero headline + opening line. Establish relevance to the reader's financial situation or goals before pitching anything.
- **Desire** — body copy. Connect the offer/content to a concrete outcome (saved time, saved money, reduced risk, increased return) using specific numbers where verified.
- **Action** — CTA. One primary action per email. Competing CTAs measurably reduce conversion in financial services email (multiple studies on bank marketing consistently show single-CTA emails outperform multi-CTA by wide margins).

## Psychological principles (use deliberately, name the one you're using)

1. **Authority** — cite credentials, regulatory compliance, years in business, AUM, or third-party ratings. Finance readers are risk-averse; authority signals reduce perceived risk.
2. **Social proof** — customer counts, testimonials, "join X other businesses." Works especially well for SMB banking and lending products where the reader is evaluating unfamiliar vendors.
3. **Loss aversion** — often more effective than gain-framing in finance ("stop losing $40/month in hidden fees" beats "save $40/month"). Use for retention, fee transparency, and fraud-protection messaging.
4. **Reciprocity** — free tools, calculators, educational content, or assessments offered before the ask. Effective in top-of-funnel/educational newsletters.
5. **Scarcity/urgency** — use only when genuinely true (real deadline, real limited allocation). Fabricated urgency is a major trust violator in a category built on trust; flag to the user if they request fake urgency.
6. **Specificity/concreteness** — "reduce reconciliation time by 3.2 hours/month" beats "save time." Numbers increase perceived credibility even before the reader verifies them.
7. **Simplicity/clarity bias** — financial products are inherently complex; the newsletter that explains the complex thing most simply wins trust. Prefer one clear idea over three hedged ones.

## Fintech-specific tactics

- **Regulatory reassurance**: For deposit products, reference FDIC/NCUA insurance where applicable. For investment products, include required risk disclosures. This is not just compliance — properly placed reassurance *increases* conversion because it pre-empts the reader's objection.
- **Trust signal placement**: Put trust signals (security badges, regulatory mentions, third-party validation) near the CTA, not just in the footer — readers look for reassurance at the moment of decision.
- **Complexity simplification**: Use one analogy or one concrete example rather than explaining a financial mechanism abstractly. E.g., explain compound interest via a specific dollar example, not the formula.
- **Segment by financial sophistication**: Retail/consumer audiences need more explanation and reassurance; advisor/institutional audiences want density and want jargon (it signals competence, not confusion, to that audience).
- **Avoid manufactured scarcity on regulated products**: "Only 3 spots left" on a lending product can trigger regulatory scrutiny (implies unequal access) — steer users away from this pattern for credit/lending copy specifically.

## Subject line formulas that work in finance

- **Specific benefit + timeframe**: "Cut your card processing fees by 15% this quarter"
- **Curiosity + relevance**: "The rate change most homeowners are missing"
- **Direct value**: "Your Q3 portfolio summary is ready"
- **Question that implies a gap**: "Is your business overpaying on payroll taxes?"
- Avoid: ALL CAPS, excessive punctuation, "free," "guarantee," "act now" — these trip spam filters in financial services email specifically (higher scrutiny than general commerce email).

## CTA copy patterns

- Prefer specific action + implied value: "See My Rate" > "Learn More"
- First-person CTA copy ("Start My Application") often outperforms third-person ("Start Your Application") in financial services — reduces friction by making the action feel already-decided.
- Secondary CTA (if used) should be lower-commitment: "Not ready? Download the rate guide" rather than a second competing primary action.
